What is the state, and how does it change?

My research is about learning state representations for biological dynamical systems. A brain or an embryo is only seen through measurements that are high-dimensional, noisy, and partial, and labels are scarce. I build models that recover the underlying state from such data and follow how it evolves, and I test the methods first where the true state is known.

Brain dynamics and brain-computer interfaces

EEG and intracranial recordings are noisy and high-dimensional, and they vary across people, sessions, and devices, so the quality of the learned brain-state representation decides what a decoder can do. In the AI-Mind project I was responsible for the machine learning on high-density EEG from 1,000 participants in four countries, where self-supervised pre-training raised balanced accuracy by more than 10% in some experimental designs. As algorithm lead at Neuralicorn, I led a speech-decoding experiment for an implantable BCI, from the experimental paradigm to clinical data collection in hospital.

  • Speech decoding for implantable BCIs
  • Self-supervision for EEG and neural signals
  • Longitudinal brain-network dynamics
  • Cross-subject and cross-device robustness

Embryo development and genomics

In the GENESIS convergence environment at the University of Oslo, I am responsible for designing the machine-learning models for gene perturbation, which model how perturbing a gene shifts cell and embryo states. I also develop deep-learning methods for early-embryo data: single-cell transcriptomics, spatially resolved gene expression, epigenetic signatures, and live microscopy. The aim is to identify the gene-expression modules and epigenetic signatures that organize the earliest stages of mammalian development, and to connect them with models of cell behaviour and tissue mechanics. In the Virtual Cell Challenge 2026, run by the Arc Institute, our team predicted responses to gene knockdowns in cell lines never seen in training and finished 41st of 807. The lesson carries over to embryo data. Effects measured in related systems are a strong baseline, and a foundation model earns credit only for what it adds against a matched control.

  • Gene-perturbation modelling
  • Knockdown-response prediction across cell lines
  • Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics
  • Epigenetic signatures and chromatin accessibility
  • Live-microscopy and cell-behaviour analysis

State representation learning

The methods come from my PhD. I work across the machine-learning toolbox, with a core in self-supervised and representation learning, reinforcement learning, and geometry-aware modeling. The recurring goal is a learning objective aligned with the structure of the problem: contrastive and masked objectives, manifold-aware embedding spaces, and representations that hold up under partial observability, limited memory, and the need to explore. Atari games are the test bed because their true state is known, so a learned representation can be measured against it.

  • Self-supervised and contrastive objectives
  • Geometry-aware target spaces
  • Partially observable reinforcement learning
  • Ensemble diversity and deep exploration
  • Transformer backbones for vision and control
