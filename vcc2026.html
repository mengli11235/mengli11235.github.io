Virtual Cell Challenge 2026 · Arc Institute · Team UiO

Predicting 300 gene knockdowns in cell lines we had never seen perturbed.

For each of three new cell lines we had only its control cells, and had to predict how it responds to 300 CRISPRi knockdowns. Our entry moves effects measured in public screens onto each line's own controls, and judges every learned part against a matched control. It finished 41st of 807.

The task

Challenge website

The Virtual Cell Challenge 2026 asked teams to predict, zero-shot, how three human cell lines respond to CRISPRi knockdowns. Nothing about the perturbed cells was given. Each line came only with its unperturbed controls, and the prediction had to be a full set of single cells, scored by the evaluator cell-eval2.

Validation lines
Cal-33, HeLa, Jurkat. Scored on a public board that froze on 22 October.
Test lines
AC16, MOLM-13, U-87 MG. Scored once, on each team's final entry.
What made transfer possible
All but one of the 300 test targets had been knocked down before in some other cell line.

A copy of the measurements is hard to beat

Before building anything, we tried perturbation models as predictors. On our first local fold, copying the measured effects of the same knockdowns in another line scored 0.286. Stack, a model trained to predict such effects, scored below zero, both zero-shot and fine-tuned. State's perturbation model landed about 0.39 below the plain mean of the measured tables.

So we built on measured effects, and asked every learned part to prove itself against them.

How the entry works

AtlasShift, a public baseline under the MIT licence, gave us the skeleton: effects measured in public screens, carried onto each line's control cells, and a generator that draws the cells. All our changes sit between its profile step and its generator.

OursAtlasShift's

  1. 1 · AtlasShift

    Measured effects

    Per-knockdown effects from public screens, in a mean space and a bulk space, less the response common to all knockdowns.

  2. 2 · Ours

    Noise-aware tables

    Each source is shrunk by its own sampling noise, then sources are fused by direction so each has the same say.

  3. 3 · Ours

    Completing missing genes

    The K562 screen measures 7,681 genes. A regression on the new line's control axes fills the rest, where 47% of the truth's energy lay in our folds.

  4. 4 · AtlasShift

    Desired profiles

    Effects become target profiles on the new line's controls, with a promoter prior and a CD4 T-cell family.

  5. 5 · Ours

    Foundation-model heads

    TabPFN heads on frozen embeddings from 16 foundation models. They may only amplify changes the table already makes.

  6. 6 · Ours

    Bulk path

    Remove 15 control axes, set a gain per line, mix with a pooled table, and add direction-only tables from external predictors such as UPM and PIE.

  7. 7 · Ours

    Call list and floor

    Genes just past a per-gene detection line are lifted in the predicted direction. Listed genes whose direction disagrees are set to no change.

  8. 8 · Ours

    Fill rule

    Each line's amplitude is set from an estimate of the capped MSE's budget-to-claim ratio, explained below.

  9. 9 · AtlasShift

    Count generator

    Draws 400 cells per knockdown from the new line's own controls to match both profiles.

Five measured sources. K562 genome-wide screen (weight 2), HCT116 and HEK293T from X-Atlas/Orion (1 each), the 2025 challenge's H1 cells (2) and KOLF2.1J (1).

Filling to the kink

The mse metric gives a prediction a sampling correction, capped by a budget equal to the prediction's own spread across knockdowns. In a toy model with one direction, extra amplitude below the cap costs nothing, and only its alignment with the truth counts. A weakly aligned prediction should therefore be filled exactly to the kink.

Our fill rule estimates the budget-to-claim ratio from the submission alone and scales each line toward 1.04. An earlier package sat at about 0.72, well below the kink.

Results

  • One of a band. Thirteen other entries lie within 0.004 of ours, ranks 33 to 46. Redrawing an entry's cells moved its score by up to 0.004.
  • Validation board. Our best upload scored 0.1882, rank 347 of 1,635 on the frozen board.
  • Forecast. Our local folds had predicted about 0.32. The forecast rested on one pair of scores and on folds reused for hundreds of comparisons.

What we learned

1

Measured effects, carried over with care, do most of the work.

All three winning entries share our structure: effects measured in other cell lines, adapted to the new line through its control cells. The organisers concluded that reference data and the care of its transfer counted at least as much as architecture.

2

Test data the way it will be used.

We added public tables one at a time to a fixed average. One table moves an average little, so each read a few thousandths and was dropped. Data meant for a model should go in as a batch, with whole cell lines held out.

3

A model earns credit only against a control of the same form.

A head of the same form without the single-cell embeddings added as much as one with them. Without that control we would have credited the embeddings.

4

Ask why a model predicts what it does.

PIE's detection probabilities led us to the call floor, which stayed in the entry after PIE's own directions had been replaced.

What we would change

These follow from comparing our entry with the winners. None of them was run, and we do not know their size.

  1. More practice lines, from more than one lab

    Two of our three folds came from one lab and shared 299 of 300 targets. Every learned step was selected on what amounts to two cell contexts.

  2. A response model trained across many public screens

    The second-place entry learned from 86 cell contexts with whole contexts held out. Our heads were fitted on seven.

  3. Source weights from control similarity

    The first- and third-place entries weight each reference by how close its controls are to the new line. We tried it with four sources and kept fixed weights.

  4. More public data, used as training contexts

    We judged about 30 further tables, most of them one at a time, and kept none.

Where we would look first

The mse term carries 37% of our distance to first place, and it reads the size of every predicted change. Our sizes came from rules: one gain per line and a fill computed from the metric's budget. A size learned per gene and knockdown is the first thing we would try.

Built on others' work

AtlasShift (MIT) gave the skeleton and the count generator. cell-eval2 is the evaluator, and our fill rule uses a property of its capped MSE. Sidechain's pooled posterior sets the direction of the call list. The idea of calls just past the detection line also appears in the public T3V200 recipe, and the idea of removing control axes came from board notes of several teams. The foundation models, TabPFN, ESM-2, GenePT, UPM, PIE and every dataset belong to their authors. The final entry contains numbers from models under non-commercial licences, so it is for non-commercial use. Our code is MIT.

Thanks to the Arc Institute for running the challenge, and to the teams who shared notes on the board.
